# Preparing for Work and Life in 2030

Research reviewed on 15 October 2026. This is scenario planning for FDE Academy, separating observed evidence, conditional forecasts, and curriculum design choices.

The strongest course direction is to combine conceptual understanding, effective use of powerful AI, and experience producing outcomes in a real domain. Prepare for AI to perform implementation, architecture, research, testing, planning, and much of coordination. Develop the learner's ability to understand and direct an entire activity as the available automation changes.

No course can guarantee employment if broadly capable AI becomes a cheap substitute for most knowledge work. Preparation can improve a person's options, agency, and ability to adapt; employment also depends on adoption, institutions, demand, and how gains are distributed.

## Evidence available in October 2026

### Capabilities are advancing across several kinds of work

Stanford's April 2026 AI Index summary describes progress across reasoning, coding agents, and scientific applications, alongside uneven performance. This is evidence of broadening capability, rather than a date for universal competence. See [the 2026 AI Index takeaways](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/inside-the-ai-index-12-takeaways-from-the-2026-report).

METR measures software-task completion against human task duration at specified success rates. Its task suite is cleaner and lower-context than much actual employment, and its current page warns that measurements above sixteen hours are unreliable. Do not extrapolate benchmark trends mechanically into a date when whole jobs disappear. See [METR's measurements and limitations](https://metr.org/time-horizons/).

**Curriculum implication:** assume much stronger agents can arrive, and keep delegating newly automatable work. Assess complete outcomes and conceptual transfer instead of preserving today's manual steps.

### Early career pressure deserves attention

Stanford's August 2026 revision of its employment study uses US payroll data through June 2026. Employment among people aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations stood about 19% below a comparison path based on less-exposed peers. The authors explicitly describe these patterns as observational, not causal estimates of AI's effect, and report no widespread economy-wide displacement. See [the authors' revised findings](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/).

The ILO's May 2025 analysis estimates occupational exposure across countries; roughly one quarter of global employment falls within its exposure categories. Exposure describes tasks potentially affected, rather than a count of jobs already lost. See [the ILO methodology and findings](https://www.ilo.org/resource/article/how-might-generative-ai-impact-different-occupations).

**Curriculum implication:** give beginners experience with an entire useful workflow, unfamiliar requests, and feedback. Do not rely on the availability of routine junior implementation work as their only entry route.

### Tool adoption and useful organizational adoption differ

The OECD's 2026 D4SME survey describes increasing AI use but uneven integration into business operations, with skills, time, maintenance, and cybersecurity barriers. Its sample exceeds 2,000 SMEs in twelve OECD countries and is not representative. See [Empowering SMEs in the age of AI](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/empowering-smes-in-the-age-of-ai_bf5a9816-en.html).

**Curriculum implication:** practise understanding workflows, selecting an existing product versus building, integrating with current systems, introducing a change, and measuring whether people benefit. Treat these as relevant work today and a candidate source of future work, rather than a permanent barrier AI cannot cross.

### Building successfully and learning successfully are different outcomes

In a January 2026 randomized study of 52 developers learning an unfamiliar Python library, the AI group averaged 50% on an immediate quiz versus 67% in the comparison group: a seventeen percentage-point difference. This small, short experiment does not establish long-term outcomes or effects for every tool or learner. Exploratory interaction patterns associated comprehension-seeking with stronger results, without proving that mechanism causally. See [the study and its limitations](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills).

**Curriculum implication:** make concepts compulsory while allowing AI implementation. Ask for predictions, explanations, changed scenarios, experiments, and later revisits. Measure understanding separately from a finished screen.

### Employers anticipate substantial skill change

The World Economic Forum's January 2025 survey reports that employers expect 39% of core skills to change by 2030. Analytical thinking, adaptability, AI, data, and cybersecurity feature in the outlook. These are surveyed expectations, not verified future job counts. See [the skills outlook](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/in-full/3-skills-outlook/).

**Curriculum implication:** conceptual transfer, communication, and the ability to learn a new system belong in assessments alongside product delivery.

### AI availability will be shaped by physical and regional conditions

The IEA's 2026 central projection puts data-centre electricity consumption at about 950 TWh in 2030, up from 485 TWh in 2025, and discusses infrastructure bottlenecks. This is a conditional energy projection, not a fixed limit on intelligence. See [Key Questions on Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary).

The World Bank's November 2025 analysis emphasizes connectivity, compute, local context and data, and competency. It documents uneven access and the relevance of local language and lightweight applications. See [Strengthening AI Foundations](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2025/11/21/strengthening-ai-foundations-emerging-opportunities-for-developing-countries).

**Curriculum implication:** include resource constraints, local users and languages, changing prices, provider portability, and data ownership. Avoid assuming that a frontier lab's capabilities describe what every learner or business can access.

### Physical automation is growing on its own deployment path

IFR reported in September 2026 that the operational industrial robot stock reached about five million units in 2025. That concerns installed factory robots, not general household autonomy or a forecast that physical jobs are protected. See [World Robotics 2026 findings](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/five-million-robots-now-operate-in-factories-globally).

**Curriculum implication:** offer domain projects involving buildings, maintenance, logistics, energy, and equipment as well as digital services. Teach the connection between software representations, actual conditions, people, and devices. Robotics depth can remain a specialist option.

### Verification and authority become everyday literacy

The International AI Safety Report 2026 documents AI-assisted fraud and impersonation, cyber risks, and automation bias; it also identifies important evidence gaps. Its findings concern observed risks and available studies, rather than proof that future systems must remain unreliable. See [the international scientific assessment](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026).

**Curriculum implication:** teach provenance, identity checks, permissions, privacy, and authority over actions. Let AI perform verification work while checking that the evidence concerns the correct objective and source.

### Economic outcomes remain conditional

Anthropic's September 2026 economic explorer presents modest, substantial, and extreme US scenarios. In the most transformative case, broad autonomous knowledge work brings strong growth alongside severe labour-market disruption. The model omits important mechanisms, including advanced robotics and policy responses; its scenarios are not assigned probabilities. See [the model, assumptions, and limitations](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios).

**Curriculum implication:** include a scenario where directing agents, planning, and evaluating are themselves substantially automated. Avoid promising that any newly named human role is permanently protected.

## Three plausible worlds to prepare for

These are curriculum stress tests informed by the evidence above, not probability forecasts or direct reproductions of any one institution's model.

| 2030 scenario | What everyday work could look like | Preparation that remains useful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Powerful tools with uneven adoption | Capable agents coexist with older systems, incomplete data, different budgets, and organizations at different adoption stages | Understand concepts, connect systems, investigate local needs, introduce change, and establish actual benefit |
| Widespread autonomous delivery | Agents handle much of product design, implementation, analysis, testing, and operations; smaller groups deliver broader workflows | Understand objectives and tradeoffs, provide access and authority, reason about evidence, and deliver outcomes in a domain |
| Broad cognitive automation | Most standard knowledge work, including much coordination and judgement, is cheap to automate; employment and institutions change substantially | Learn continuously, transfer between domains, maintain personal agency and trusted relationships, and recognize that a course alone cannot insure a career |

Resource constraints, regulation, organizational choices, and unequal access can occur in all three worlds. Capability, use, wages, and employment do not move in lockstep.

## What wider human life could look like

The following are plausible extensions, rather than established facts about 2030.

**Learning may become continuously available.** Personal AI tutors could explain, translate, simulate, and adapt practice on demand. The challenge for a learner is establishing that they can apply the concept later, including when the tutor's suggestion does not fit.

**Creating digital material may become much easier.** Websites, video, research summaries, business plans, and software could be produced with little manual implementation. If supply expands, attention, access to users, differentiation, and demonstrated usefulness may become more important to earning from that work.

**Work may be reorganized around broader outcomes.** One person with capable systems might deliver a workflow that previously involved several specialist handoffs. Some organizations may reduce hiring; others may expand output. These are different responses to the same capability.

**Science and physical industries may change unevenly.** AI-assisted discovery and robotics could produce significant benefits, while facilities, equipment, energy, standards, training, and replacement cycles shape deployment. Include real industries in the course without assuming that every physical activity is automated or protected.

**Trust may need more deliberate evidence.** Credible-looking messages and media could be easy to produce. People should understand how to verify identity, trace information to a source, and decide which actions an automated system may take.

**Greater productive capacity may coexist with difficult transitions.** Benefits depend on access, demand, ownership, institutions, and policy. Teach people to evaluate opportunities and changing requirements without promising either universal unemployment or universal prosperity.

## The person the course should develop

| Capability | Concepts that matter | Practical demonstration with AI available |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conceptual reasoning | Systems, state, data, networks, abstraction, architecture, AI behaviour | Predict a change, explain an observation, and correct a mistaken mental model |
| Evidence and quantitative judgement | Probability, base rates, sampling, false positives, uncertainty, correlation and causation | Evaluate a claim on representative cases and explain what the evidence supports |
| Domain understanding | Actual workflows, incentives, constraints, users, information quality | Investigate a real process and discover a requirement missing from the initial request |
| Effective delegation | Goals, context, task boundaries, authority, feedback, stopping | Give agents a complete outcome, permit appropriate autonomy, and respond to a changed constraint |
| Product and economic judgement | Alternatives, existing tools, costs, adoption, differentiation, distribution | Decide what to buy, configure, integrate, or build and establish why the result is useful |
| Trust and accountability | Provenance, permissions, identity, privacy, reversible actions, recovery | Verify a consequential request and demonstrate appropriate control over an automated action |
| Communication and adoption | Clear explanations, negotiation, training, feedback, handoffs | Help another person use a solution and resolve disagreement about what success means |
| Adaptation and learning | Concept transfer, documentation, experiments, reflection | Complete work with an unfamiliar tool or domain while preserving the required outcome |

These capabilities are a preparation strategy. AI may increasingly assist with or perform each activity; their value is applying understanding and automation effectively in the situation at hand.

## A day in 2030 to design backwards from

Imagine a graduate helping a building-maintenance company reduce missed service visits. This is a teaching scenario.

The graduate establishes what a missed visit means and discovers that the records mix cancellations, access problems, and genuine scheduling failures. They use AI to investigate the records and propose alternatives. They understand the data well enough to reject a misleading success rate.

Agents can create a scheduling interface, connect the available systems, plan routes, generate checks, and monitor the service. The graduate sets the goals, access rights, budget, and conditions for contacting customers or changing appointments. They can delegate routine approvals under a suitable policy as well.

The company tries the workflow. The graduate and AI investigate whether visits improve, whether customers receive useful messages, and whether the process creates extra work elsewhere. They adapt it when a new constraint appears and help staff use it.

The evidence is a useful improvement with accurate conceptual explanations. No manual implementation quota is needed.

## Concrete changes to FDE Academy

Keep the thirty-mission structure and compulsory shared concept foundation. Add the following depth inside existing missions:

- Teach data provenance, missing information, probability, base rates, and causal claims alongside AI evaluation.
- Bring a real domain into the product brief, with incentives and workflow constraints as well as screens and features.
- Compare existing tools, configuration, integration, and custom building before selecting a solution.
- Assess adoption and benefit after delivery, including effects on the rest of the workflow.
- Practise setting authority and budgets for systems capable of taking actions.
- Include local-language, access, cost, and portability constraints.
- Demonstrate identity and source verification through a relevant scenario.
- Keep AI available for implementation, planning, testing, and diagnosis. Assess conceptual understanding separately through predictions and changed situations.

Add graduation challenges where a client changes a goal, a dataset is misleading, a provider becomes unsuitable, or a convincingly presented request lacks verified authority. Choose scenarios relevant to the learner's product. Improvements in AI reliability should raise the challenge level rather than eliminate concept assessment.

Measure learners and experienced participants using comparable tools and constraints. Compare the accepted outcome, conceptual reasoning, time, cost, adoption, and recovery. Do not assume either that a graduate matches an experienced engineer or that experience automatically wins.

## Signals that should trigger a curriculum revision

| Signal | Course response |
| --- | --- |
| Agents reliably perform another activity end to end in the target setting | Delegate it and assess a broader outcome |
| Employers or clients stop paying for a familiar deliverable | Reassess target workflows, domain choices, and positioning |
| Learners produce impressive work but fail transfer questions later | Strengthen experiments, predictions, explanations, and spaced revisits |
| A new model or provider changes the economics | Refresh comparison and portability exercises |
| Customer adoption lags behind technical success | Add workflow investigation, feedback, and change practice |
| Regional access or policies change | Revise local deployment, data, and tool options |
| Broad cognitive automation substantially reduces accessible roles | Reassess the employment claim and support transitions beyond the original role |

Review these signals quarterly and before each cohort. Future relevance comes from updating the course using observed change, alongside concepts learners can transfer.
