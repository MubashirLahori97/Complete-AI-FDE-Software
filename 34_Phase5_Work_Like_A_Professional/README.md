# 💼 Phase 5 — Work Like a Professional: The Other Half of the Job *(Days 121–150)*

Code is half the job. **The other half: security, teams, clients, and communication.** This is the phase that separates "person who can code" from "person companies fight to hire."

---

## Week 1 — Security basics *(Days 121–127)*

**Goal:** stop shipping software that's easy to break into.

**Learn:** the OWASP Top 10 risks, in plain words (injection, broken auth, exposed secrets — what they are, what they look like) · secrets management (never hardcode, ever) · auth done right · what an attack actually looks like in your logs.

**Specify → AI builds:** AI attacks your app on paper — "here are 5 ways I'd break in." You fix each one, then verify the fix.

**Evaluate:** name the 3 most likely attacks on YOUR app and your defense for each. Write them down. That's your first security doc.

**💼 Business lens:** one breach ends a freelancer's reputation and can end a small company. "I security-reviewed my own work" is a sentence clients pay extra for.

**The one link:** [OWASP Top 10](https://owasp.org/www-project-top-ten/) — the industry's shared list of what goes wrong.

**Done when:**
- [ ] Security review written for your own projects, everything found is fixed
- [ ] No secrets in any repo, ever again — verified, not assumed

---

## Week 2 — Team workflows *(Days 128–134)*

**Goal:** make teams faster, not just yourself.

**Learn:** tickets and boards (what a good ticket looks like) · sprints without the theater · giving code review that helps · receiving review without ego · pair programming — with humans and with AI.

**Specify → AI builds:** run a real 2-week "sprint" on your own project: board, tickets, review, retrospective. AI plays the teammate who reviews your PRs harshly.

**Evaluate:** did the board change what you actually worked on? If yes, the process works. Write the retrospective honestly.

**💼 Business lens:** companies don't hire brilliant loners; they hire people who make the whole team faster. This week is evidence you can.

**The one link:** [GitHub Flow guide](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/using-github/github-flow) — how teams actually ship with branches and PRs.

**Done when:**
- [ ] One full sprint run on your own project, board + retrospective to prove it
- [ ] You gave (and received) at least one serious code review

---

## Week 3 — Clients and money *(Days 135–141)*

**Goal:** get paid properly for the skills you now have.

**Learn:** scoping work so it doesn't explode · pricing: hourly vs fixed vs value-based, in plain words · what a contract must contain · saying no to bad work · getting testimonials that actually sell.

**Specify → AI builds:** AI drafts your contract and your proposal; you interrogate every clause until you understand what it protects. Never sign what you can't explain.

**Evaluate:** one paid engagement, end to end, WITH a written contract — and a testimonial at the end. That's the whole week.

**💼 Business lens:** this week can pay for the entire 180 days. Pricing confidence is a skill; most developers never learn it and leave money on the table forever.

**The one link:** [Freelancers Union](https://freelancersunion.org/) — free resources on contracts, rates, and not getting exploited.

**Done when:**
- [ ] One paid engagement completed with a signed contract
- [ ] One written testimonial from a real client

---

## Week 4 — Teach to learn *(Days 142–150)*

**Goal:** turn your knowledge into proof the world can see.

**Learn:** docs people actually read · READMEs that sell · writing one public tutorial · answering one question well in public (Stack Overflow, a forum, a community).

**Specify → AI builds:** AI edits your tutorial for clarity; you keep your voice. If it sounds like a robot wrote it, rewrite it.

**Evaluate:** did a stranger say your tutorial or answer helped them? Screenshot it. That's the metric.

**💼 Business lens:** public teaching is the cheapest marketing a developer has. It also exposes every gap in your own understanding — free self-review.

**The one link:** [Google's Technical Writing course](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing) — short, free, excellent.

**Done when:**
- [ ] One public tutorial published
- [ ] One genuinely helpful public answer, with proof someone valued it

---

## 🏁 Phase project — "The Professional Pack"

Three artifacts in your repo: (1) the security audit of your own work, (2) the client engagement — contract, delivery, testimonial, and (3) your public tutorial. **Together they say: this person is safe to hire.**

---

## ⚠️ Traps to avoid

- **Security paranoia.** Learn the basics and apply them. You are not becoming a cryptographer this week.
- **Undercharging forever.** "Exposure" doesn't pay rent. Raise your price with your proof.
- **Writing instead of building.** One tutorial is proof. Ten tutorials is procrastination.

---

## 🧠 Phase check

<details>
<summary><b>Q1.</b> Why does this phase matter if you "just want a job writing code"?</summary>

Because jobs go to people companies trust — with their systems (security), their teams (workflows), their clients (professionalism), and their reputation (communication). Code gets you the interview; this gets you the offer.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q2.</b> A client asks for "a small change" mid-project. What's the professional move?</summary>

Scope it, price it, write it down, get agreement — before touching the code. "Small changes" are how fixed-price projects become unpaid months.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q3.</b> Why teach in public instead of just learning more?</summary>

Teaching forces completeness — you can't hand-wave past the part you don't understand when strangers are watching. It's the fastest way to find your own gaps.
</details>
